# <span class="cat strategy">Strategy</span> Joint functions $f(x,y)$: quantum vs classical communication

| $f(x,y)$ | model (bounded error) | qubits | bits | reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| equality $[x=y]$ | simultaneous (SMP), no shared randomness | $O(\log n)$ | $\Theta(\sqrt n)$ | Buhrman et al. 2001 |
| Hamming distance $\le d$ ($d$ fixed) | SMP, no shared randomness | $O(\log n)$ | $\Theta(\sqrt n)$ | Yao 2003 |
| disjointness $[x\cap y=\emptyset]$ | two-way | $\Theta(\sqrt n)$ | $\Theta(n)$ | Aaronson, Ambainis 2003 |
| Boolean hidden matching, variant (promise) | one-way | $O(\log n)$ | $\Theta(\sqrt n)$ | Gavinsky et al. 2007 |
| vector in subspace (promise) | quantum one-way, classical two-way | $O(\log n)$ | $\Omega(n^{1/3})$ | Klartag, Regev 2011 |
| inner product mod 2: <span class="noadv">no advantage</span> | two-way, even with entanglement | $\Theta(n)$ | $\Theta(n)$ | Cleve et al. 1998 |

- **Our architecture:** the senders hold $x$ and $y$; the receiving QPU, given no input, is the SMP referee.
- **First target, equality:** SMP matches our $2$ senders $\to$ $1$ receiver, and the referee needs only a SWAP test.
- **Measure** worst-case error vs message size, CC vs QC (trained parameters act as a shared key).

<div class="src">Buhrman, Cleve, Watrous, de Wolf, PRL 87, 167902 (2001); Yao, STOC 2003; Buhrman, Cleve, Wigderson, STOC 1998; Aaronson, Ambainis, Theory Comput. 1, 47 (2005); Razborov, Izv. Math. 67, 145 (2003); Bar-Yossef, Jayram, Kerenidis, STOC 2004; Gavinsky, Kempe, Kerenidis, Raz, de Wolf, STOC 2007; Klartag, Regev, STOC 2011; Cleve, van Dam, Nielsen, Tapp, LNCS 1509, 61 (1998).</div>

<!-- Speaker note (table): costs are the communication in qubits (quantum) or bits (classical); n = input length in bits (for the subspace problem, the dimension of x, a unit vector in R^n; y is a subspace of dimension n/2, promise x in y or in its orthogonal complement). Hamming distance: d fixed. Disjointness: x, y read as subsets of {1..n}, f = 1 iff there is no i with x_i = y_i = 1; the first quantum protocol, O(sqrt n log n), is Buhrman, Cleve, Wigderson 1998, the matching Omega(sqrt n) lower bound is Razborov 2003. Boolean hidden matching: introduced (with the relational hidden matching problem) by Bar-Yossef, Jayram, Kerenidis 2004; the one-way separation for a Boolean variant is Gavinsky et al. 2007. Vector in subspace: quantum one-way, classical lower bound for two-way protocols. SMP = simultaneous message passing: each party sends one message to a referee who holds no input; one-way = one message from Alice to Bob; two-way = interactive. All rows are bounded error (correct with probability >= 2/3 on every input), costs up to constant factors. Promise problems (partial functions) are defined only on inputs that satisfy a promise; in this table the exponential separations for one-way protocols are of this kind, and hidden matching started as a relation (several valid outputs). Equality and Hamming distance: the separation needs "no shared randomness"; with a shared random key equality costs O(1) classical bits. Inner product mod 2 is the counterexample: Theta(n) qubits even with prior entanglement, so not every joint function benefits (for exact protocols, entanglement halves the cost to ceil(n/2) qubits by superdense coding and no further; with bounded error it stays Omega(n), Cleve et al. eqs. 2-3). Not in the table: the distributed Deutsch-Jozsa problem (promise: x = y or Hamming distance n/2) needs O(log n) qubits exactly but Omega(n) bits for exact (zero-error) classical protocols (Buhrman, Cleve, Wigderson 1998, Theorem 1.7); with bounded error, classical O(log n) bits suffice, so it separates only exact protocols. Also: for almost all functions, quantum fingerprinting is exponentially worse than classical deterministic SMP (Gavinsky, Kempe, de Wolf, CCC 2006): fingerprints help for special functions such as equality and bounded Hamming distance. -->
<!-- Speaker note (architecture and first target): our default topology, two senders -> one receiver, has the shape of the SMP model once the receiving QPU gets no input of its own (in the model the referee holds no input; today our receiving QPU encodes its own feature block). Equality is the natural first target: (i) its separation is in the SMP model, which matches this topology without interaction; (ii) the referee is small: each sender sends a k-qubit fingerprint |h_x> = (1/sqrt m) sum_i (-1)^{E_i(x)} |i>, and the SWAP test needs 2k + 1 qubits (two registers and one ancilla, paper eq. 3 and Fig. 1), answering "equal" with probability (1 + |<h_x|h_y>|^2)/2; (iii) the same SWAP test, repeated on k copies, estimates the overlap |<u|v>| (Yao 2003, Lemma 1), which is how Yao's Hamming-distance protocol works, so Hamming distance is the natural second step. The disjointness protocols are interactive: every Grover query becomes a round trip of O(log n) qubits (Buhrman, Cleve, Wigderson, Theorem 2.1: t queries give a t(2 log2 N + 4)-qubit protocol), hence a two-way topology. With CC each sender transmits message bits; with QC it transmits its qubits. No communication (NC) gives the chance level. What we would learn: whether training finds fingerprint-like codes and a SWAP-test-like referee, and whether trained classical messages stay above the sqrt(n) scaling on worst-case inputs. Classical baseline: an unrestricted classical b-bit encoder of x (or the Theta(sqrt n) bound itself), not only our threshold-bit CC, because the paper's classical bound is for unrestricted classical senders while our CC sends a threshold of a K-shot estimate of the sender's encoded state; a CC-vs-QC gap measured with our CC alone could reflect the restricted sender, not the communication bound. -->
<!-- Speaker note (caveat, our analysis, not in the paper as stated): CC = classical communication, QC = quantum communication (the pooled qubits are sent). Trained parameters act as a shared key, so average test accuracy can hide the gap. For a fixed input distribution, averaging the paper's shared-key protocol over the key shows that some fixed key has average error at most the protocol's worst-case error (Yao's minimax principle; Kushilevitz and Nisan, Communication Complexity, 1997). A trained classical model has fixed parameters chosen with knowledge of the training distribution, so it can reach low average error with O(1) bits per sender; the Theta(sqrt n) bound concerns worst-case inputs. The paper itself notes that "for every fixed key value, there are distinct inputs x and y on which the protocol gives the incorrect output 1" (p. 1). Hence: evaluate on adversarially chosen pairs (worst case), not only on a fixed test set. -->
